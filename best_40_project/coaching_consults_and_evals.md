## Staying present on sales call when personal circumstances are distracting

**Date:** 2026-06-11

**QUESTION:**

Hi there, I had a sales call over the weekend that didn’t close, and I know why. There was a money objection, that likely was hiding an underlying fear about her confidence in her ability to get the result (enough to make it worth the investment), but I struggled to stay with her on the call because of some life circumstances that were happening at the same time. I’d like support on how to stay present even when other things are going on.

Circumstances: my husband and I had had a fight the day before, and I’d left the fight feeling like he wasn’t supportive of my business. We didn’t talk it through before the sales call (we ended up discussing later that day and moving through it), and so when the client brought up money, I felt discouraged because that keeps being an objection I’m getting, I was annoyed with my husband for not being supportive, and I definitely just started believing her objection. It was hard to rein myself in in the moment.

Additionally, right before the call, my son woke up from his nap with an ear infection. My husband was with him and ended up needing to take him to urgent care. My husband texted me a couple updates during the sales call, and so that was understandably distracting as I was trying to focus on this potential client. I’m confident in my husband’s ability to care for our son, so I didn’t feel the need to cut the call short, and I didn’t think I needed to be with my son right in that moment. But there was some guilt about staying on the call, wondering if I was doing something wrong, wondering how to balance business priorities with motherhood. But if I compare it to a day job, if I were in an important work meeting, I would likely trust my husband to handle things and stay in the meeting. But my son not being well also played into the situation with my husband and not feeling supported from him.

I’d love some coaching on staying present on a sales call when other urgent life things feel distracting, and also that balance of prioritizing the business when my son isn’t feeling well. I think there’s mom guilt getting in the way, so that feels like a different issue than the husband situation. Would love your thoughts!

**ANSWER:**

Hello Coach,

Thank you for sharing this and being willing to bring it here. It sounds like there was a lot happening all at once, and I want to acknowledge that. You were then faced with an objection that has already been feeling charged for you lately. 
That is a lot.

And before we coach this, I want to offer you some compassion. We have all been there. Whether it’s a consult, a coaching call, or an important conversation, sometimes life is happening at the same time. Would you really expect yourself to be completely undistracted with everything you had going on?

I think the first place I would start is accepting that life will challenge us. Being a coach doesn’t mean you never get distracted. Being a coach doesn’t mean you never have emotions. Nothing has gone wrong here.

Sometimes we think the goal is to show up perfectly focused no matter what is happening in our lives. But what if the goal is simply learning how to bring yourself back when you notice you’ve left the room mentally?

One thing I’m curious about is this: 
If you expected yourself not to be distracted, why?

What are you making distraction mean?

I’m asking because sometimes we suffer more from the judgment of the distraction than the distraction itself.

What if you accepted that sometimes you might show up a little distracted? Or even a lot distracted? What would you want to make that mean about yourself and your ability to be present?

I think there is something powerful about giving yourself the same grace we encourage our clients to have. You can be a great coach and still be human. You can be present and still have emotions. You can be thinking about your child and still be a professional.

You mentioned that when the money objection came up, you started believing it. That stood out to me. 
You said: 
“I felt discouraged because that keeps being an objection I’m getting.”

So I would get curious there.

Why do money objections discourage you?

What thought is creating the discouragement?

Are you making money objections mean something about your offer?

Something about your business?

Something about you?

I’m asking because if we solve for the discouragement, we often solve for a lot of what happens on the consult.

When we believe the objection ourselves, it becomes harder to stay curious.

If you had been feeling completely grounded and present, what would you have wanted to ask her about the money objection?

What would you have been curious to understand?

Sometimes after a consult, it can be helpful to replay it and ask yourself, “Where would I have gotten more curious if I wasn’t believing the objection myself?”

The other piece that stood out to me was your husband.

You mentioned feeling unsupported by him and then feeling discouraged by the client’s objection.

I wonder if there is any connection there.

Why do you need your husband to believe in the business in order for you to believe in the business?

What happens if he doesn’t?

What are you making his lack of support mean?

I’m asking because many of us unintentionally borrow confidence from other people. We think their belief creates our belief. But ultimately, your belief has to come from you.

And then there is the mom guilt piece.

I noticed you said that if you were at a traditional job in an important meeting, you likely would have trusted your husband to handle it and stayed in the meeting.

That makes me wonder:

What makes this different because it’s your business or how you think about that?

What are you making it mean when you prioritize your business while your child is being cared for by someone you trust?

Sometimes guilt feels like evidence that we’re doing something wrong. 
But guilt is often just a feeling.

It doesn’t mean you’re doing something wrong.

It may simply mean you’re challenging an old belief about what a good mother is supposed to do.

And finally, I want to offer this:

You don’t have to become someone who never gets distracted. 
You simply practice noticing when your brain leaves the conversation and bringing it back.

“My brain is on my son.” 
Come back. 
“My brain is on my husband.” 
Come back. 
“My brain is believing the money objection.” 
Over and over. 
That is the skill.

And like any skill, you get better with practice.

I would spend less time trying not to get distracted and more time learning how to return.

What thoughts come up for you as you sit with these questions?  You got this Coach!
